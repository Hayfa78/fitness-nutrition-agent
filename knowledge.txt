# SECTION: Calorie Deficit and Weight Loss
Weight loss requires a calorie deficit — consuming fewer calories than you burn.
A moderate deficit of 300–500 kcal/day leads to sustainable fat loss of 0.25–0.5 kg per week.
Extreme deficits (>700 kcal/day) risk muscle loss, metabolic adaptation, and nutrient deficiencies.
Combining a moderate deficit with strength training helps preserve lean muscle while losing fat.
Tracking calories even roughly improves adherence and outcomes.

# SECTION: Safe Calorie Deficits
A safe calorie deficit is typically 10–20% below your TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure).
For most adults: 300–500 kcal/day deficit for fat loss, 200–300 kcal/day surplus for muscle gain.
Never eat below your BMR unless under medical supervision — this risks metabolic slowdown.
Very low calorie diets (under 800 kcal/day) should only be followed under clinical supervision.
A deficit of 500 kcal/day creates approximately 0.5 kg of fat loss per week (3,500 kcal = ~0.45 kg fat).
Refeeds every 1–2 weeks at maintenance calories help prevent metabolic adaptation during long cuts.

# SECTION: Protein Intake
Protein is essential for muscle repair, growth, immune function, and satiety.
General recommendation: 1.6–2.2 g of protein per kg of body weight per day for active individuals.
For fat loss: aim for the higher end (2.0–2.4 g/kg) to preserve muscle while in a deficit.
For muscle gain: 1.6–2.0 g/kg is sufficient; more than 2.4 g/kg shows diminishing returns.
Sedentary adults need around 0.8 g/kg (minimum RDA), but active people benefit from more.
Good protein sources: chicken breast, eggs, Greek yogurt, tofu, lentils, fish, cottage cheese, whey.
Spreading protein across 3–5 meals (20–40 g per meal) maximises muscle protein synthesis.

# SECTION: Macronutrient Ratios
A balanced macronutrient ratio depends on goals:
- Fat loss: 30–40% protein, 20–30% fat, 30–40% carbohydrates
- Muscle gain: 25–35% protein, 20–30% fat, 40–50% carbohydrates
- Endurance: 15–20% protein, 20–25% fat, 55–65% carbohydrates
- General health: 20–30% protein, 25–35% fat, 40–55% carbohydrates
Carbohydrates are the primary fuel for high-intensity exercise; don't eliminate them for athletic goals.
Dietary fat supports hormone production, fat-soluble vitamin absorption, and joint health.
Fibre (25–35 g/day) improves satiety, digestion, and blood sugar stability.

# SECTION: Progressive Overload
Progressive overload is the gradual increase of stress placed on the body during training.
It is the primary driver of strength, muscle, and endurance gains over time.
Methods to apply progressive overload:
1. Increase load (weight) by 2.5–5% when you can complete all reps with good form.
2. Add 1–2 reps per set before increasing weight.
3. Add a set (e.g., 3×10 → 4×10) to increase volume.
4. Reduce rest time between sets.
5. Increase training frequency (add a session per week).
Beginners can progress weekly; intermediate lifters every 2 weeks; advanced lifters monthly.
Always prioritise technique — progress with sloppy form leads to injury, not gains.
Deload every 4–8 weeks: reduce volume or intensity by 40–50% to allow full recovery.

# SECTION: Training Frequency
Training frequency refers to how often a muscle group or movement pattern is trained per week.
Beginners: 2–3 full-body sessions per week is optimal for learning and adaptation.
Intermediate: 3–4 sessions with upper/lower or push/pull splits.
Advanced: 4–6 sessions with dedicated muscle-group splits or frequency-focused programming.
Each muscle group benefits from being trained 2× per week for hypertrophy.
Allow at least 48 hours of recovery between sessions targeting the same muscle group.
More is not always better — recovery is when adaptation occurs, not during training.
Signs of overtraining: persistent fatigue, declining performance, mood changes, sleep disruption.

# SECTION: Recovery and Sleep
Sleep is the most powerful recovery tool available — 7–9 hours per night is the target for adults.
During deep sleep, growth hormone is released, driving muscle repair and fat metabolism.
Consistently sleeping under 6 hours impairs strength, reaction time, and hormone balance.
Active recovery (light walking, stretching, yoga) on rest days promotes blood flow without adding stress.
Foam rolling and massage reduce muscle soreness and improve range of motion.
Cold water immersion (10–15 min at 10–15°C) can reduce acute muscle soreness but may blunt adaptation.
Nutrition for recovery: prioritise carbohydrates to refill glycogen and protein (20–40 g) post-workout.
Chronic stress elevates cortisol, which impairs recovery, suppresses immunity, and promotes fat storage.
Mental recovery (downtime, social connection, hobbies) is as important as physical rest.

# SECTION: Hydration Science
Water makes up 60–70% of body weight and is critical for every physiological process.
Even mild dehydration (1–2% body weight loss) reduces strength, endurance, and cognitive performance.
General guideline: 35 ml of water per kg of body weight per day as a baseline.
Add 500–750 ml for every hour of moderate-intensity exercise; more in heat or humidity.
Electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium) are lost in sweat and must be replenished during prolonged exercise.
Pale yellow urine indicates adequate hydration; dark yellow/amber indicates dehydration.
Sports drinks are beneficial for sessions over 60–90 minutes; water is sufficient for shorter workouts.
Caffeine and alcohol are diuretics — add extra water when consuming them.
Thirst is a reliable but lagging indicator; drink consistently throughout the day rather than waiting for thirst.

# SECTION: Injury Prevention
Warming up for 5–10 minutes (light cardio + dynamic stretches) reduces injury risk.
Progressive loading — not jumping too quickly in weight or volume — is the single best injury preventer.
Pain during exercise is a warning signal — stop and assess; do not train through sharp or joint pain.
Mobility work (hip flexors, shoulders, thoracic spine) prevents compensatory movement patterns.
Adequate sleep and nutrition are critical for tissue repair; under-recovered tissue is more injury-prone.
Overuse injuries develop gradually — watch for persistent soreness in a specific area over multiple sessions.
Core stability training (planks, dead bugs, bird dogs) protects the spine during compound lifts.
Use proper footwear and equipment suited to your activity.
Listen to your body: a rest day is not a setback — it is part of the training plan.
If pain persists for more than 3–5 days, consult a physiotherapist or sports medicine professional.

# SECTION: BMI Ranges
BMI (Body Mass Index) = weight (kg) / height (m)²
BMI categories (WHO standard):
- Below 18.5: Underweight
- 18.5–24.9: Healthy weight
- 25.0–29.9: Overweight
- 30.0–34.9: Obesity class I
- 35.0–39.9: Obesity class II
- 40.0 and above: Obesity class III (severe)
BMI has limitations: it does not account for muscle mass, bone density, age, or fat distribution.
Athletes with high muscle mass often have a BMI in the overweight range despite being healthy.
A better indicator of health risk is waist circumference: above 88 cm (women) or 102 cm (men) raises risk.
Use BMI alongside other metrics (body fat %, blood markers, fitness tests) for a complete picture.

# SECTION: Strength Training Fundamentals
Compound movements (squat, deadlift, bench press, overhead press, row) build the most muscle and strength.
Isolation exercises (curls, extensions) complement compound lifts for targeted development.
Rep ranges and their general effects:
- 1–5 reps at 85–100% 1RM: maximal strength
- 6–12 reps at 67–85% 1RM: hypertrophy (muscle growth)
- 13–20+ reps at below 67% 1RM: muscular endurance
Volume (sets × reps × load) is the key driver of hypertrophy — aim for 10–20 working sets per muscle group per week.
Rest periods: 2–5 minutes for strength; 60–90 seconds for hypertrophy; 30–60 seconds for endurance.
Consistency over months and years matters far more than any individual session.

# SECTION: Cardio and Endurance Training
Cardiovascular exercise improves heart health, increases calorie burn, and boosts mood via endorphins.
Zone 2 cardio (60–70% max heart rate): sustainable, conversational pace; builds aerobic base and fat oxidation.
HIIT (High-Intensity Interval Training): alternates intense bursts with recovery; efficient for time-pressed schedules.
150 minutes of moderate-intensity or 75 minutes of vigorous cardio per week is the WHO recommendation for health.
Cardio should complement, not replace, strength training — both together produce the best health outcomes.
For fat loss, total calorie burn matters more than cardio type; do what you will sustain long term.
Overusing high-intensity cardio while in a calorie deficit risks muscle loss — keep some sessions easy.
